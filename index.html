Jury convicts man of starting the fire that became the Palisades Fire

A jury in Los Angeles on Friday convicted Jonathan Rinderknecht of starting a fire on Jan. 1, 2025, that prosecutors say flared up again as the Palisades Fire, AP and CBS Los Angeles report. The charges were destruction of property by means of fire and arson. His first trial ended in a mistrial in June.

Still unknown: No sentencing date has been reported. CBS reports he faces 5 to 40 years in federal prison.

PBS (AP)CBS Los Angeles

Context

The first jury deadlocked 10-2 in favor of acquittal, AP reports, and prosecutors then narrowed the charges. Defense attorney Steve Haney argued the evidence proved nothing and pointed to fireworks as another possible cause, AP reports. Rinderknecht’s father called the case a “false narrative,” CBS reports. The fire destroyed neighborhoods in Los Angeles and Malibu in January 2025.

Appeals court pauses the Army’s plan to blow up unstable explosives in Iowa

A federal appeals court put on hold the EPA permit the Army needs to detonate barrels of lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant near Middletown, AP and CBS report. Environmental groups had sued. The Army had already moved the detonation from Friday to Monday, and the court’s pause has no end date, CBS reports.

Reports differ: When the court acted. AP says late Thursday; CBS says Friday.

PBS (AP)CBS Chicago

Context

Lead azide is a chemical used in making ammunition that can go off easily. It had degraded in storage and become unstable, AP and CBS report. Setting it off could spread lead dust, which is toxic, CBS reports. Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle said colder weather could make the material less stable, AP reports. The groups argue other ways to get rid of it were not fully considered.

Context

How this is made

  • Claude, an AI, opens the articles and writes each story in its own words. It can make mistakes, so every story links to its sources.
  • A fact goes in only if two separate outlets report it. If only one does, the story says so. Two outlets running the same wire story count as one.
  • Before an edition goes up, a separate Claude session that didn’t write it rechecks every story against the articles. No person reads it first, so mistakes can still get through.
  • Context notes explain the background in plainer words. Claude writes them from the articles and long-settled facts, and they don’t have their own source links.
  • Claims and opinions get a name on them. Describing words like “stunning” are not allowed.
  • Choosing which stories to include is still a judgment call. That part can’t be fully objective.
  • AP, Reuters, BBC, The Guardian and The Wall Street Journal block Claude’s search tool, so they are only used where another outlet carries their stories.
